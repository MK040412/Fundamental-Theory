TL;DR. Given a million images and nothing else, how can we write down a probability distribution that we can sample from, evaluate, and fit, without giving up the flexibility to describe real data? For thirty years every model family answered by giving something up. Diffusion models answer by splitting the problem into a thousand tiny Gaussian steps, each trivially tractable, whose composition is as flexible as the network inside it. This series explains how, and it is built so that you never have to take an equation on faith:

  • Visual first. Every equation in the series gets a figure before it gets a derivation. Part 1 alone has nine.
  • Question driven. Each section is a question a reader actually asks, answered in its first sentence.
  • Reproducible. Every figure is generated by a short script in the repo, most of them on one 2-D toy dataset, so you can change the data and watch what breaks.

Diffusion is the only column that is flexible, stable to train, covers all modes, produces high-quality samples, and makes conditioning easy; the one open circle, slow sampling, is the gap that later work attacks. Introduction

Q. What does a generative model do?
A. It draws new samples, images, sentences, molecules, as if from the distribution our data came from.

The catch is that we never see that distribution, only a few samples of it (Figure 1). Classical statistics would assume a known shape, a Gaussian say, so that the prior and the posterior stay in one family we can write down. Images come with no such family: the right shape cannot be written down, and the shapes we can write down are wrong.

Remark. A generative model mimics sampling from a distribution we do not know. The sampling is the goal; the not knowing is the difficulty.

Figure 1. Samples, not a density. Left: a generative model is a sampler for a distribution \(p(x)\) over the output space. Right: all we ever see of \(p(x)\) is a handful of samples. The one shape we know how to write down, a Gaussian (gray), puts its mass where there is no data; the shape that fits, the red one, is the one we cannot write down.

Q. Then why did diffusion models appear?
A. Because every earlier model had to choose: a shape we can write down (tractable) or a shape that can be right (flexible). Diffusion gets both (Figure 2).

It does so by writing a path instead of a shape. We cannot write down the data distribution, but we can destroy it: add a little Gaussian noise, step after step, until only pure noise is left, a prior we can write. And if each step is small, undoing it is also a Gaussian (Feller, 1949): a posterior we can write, one per step, which is all the network learns. One step is a Gaussian, so it is tractable; a thousand steps composed take any shape, so it is flexible. One Gaussian at a time. The rest of this post earns that sentence.

Figure 2. One Gaussian at a time. Top: a model that writes one shape must pick. The gray Gaussian we can write down is the wrong shape; the red shape that is right cannot be written down. Bottom: diffusion writes a path between the same two shapes. Right to left, a Gaussian step we choose, \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\), adds a little noise until the data \(x_0\) has become the gray Gaussian \(x_T\). Left to right, each step back, \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\), is a Gaussian too, and that is all the network learns. Every step is tractable; the whole path is flexible.

Q. A network is a function, one output for each input. But a noisy \(x_t\) could have come from many clean \(x_0\). How can a function undo noise?
A. By asking only one step back. Where \(x_t\) came from has many answers; where it was one small step earlier has one (Figure 3).

Bayes rule splits “where was it before?” into two factors: where the noise lets it have been, \(q(x_t\mid x_s)\), and where data is common, \(q(x_s)\). The answer, \(q(x_s\mid x_t)\), is their product (Figure 3, a 1-D toy with cats and dogs).

  1. All the way back, the data decides. Many steps of noise make the noise factor a wide window, and both the cat and the dog show through it: two answers. A network trained with squared error returns their average, \(\mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t]\), which falls between them, where there is no data.
  2. One step back, the noise decides. One step moves \(x\) by only about \(\sqrt{\beta}\), so the window is narrow, and under a narrow window the data is nearly flat. The answer takes the window’s shape, one Gaussian, \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t) \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\,\beta)\), and its average \(\mu_\theta(x_t,t)\) is the answer, which regression learns. This is what the 2015 paper takes from Feller (1949): “if \(\beta_t\) is small, then \(q(x^{(t-1)}\mid x^{(t)})\) will also be a Gaussian,” so “only the mean and covariance … need be estimated.”

Figure 4 shows the same thing in two dimensions, on the 2015 paper’s own example, a swiss roll. What is Gaussian is one point’s step, not the data (left): each \(x_t\) lands in its own small round Gaussian, \(\mathcal{N}(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\,\beta I)\), whose width is the noise we chose and whose center, a little closer to the roll, is all the network predicts. Put all the points together and their small Gaussians add up to the next distribution, \(p_{t-1}(x_{t-1}) = \int q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\,p_t(x_t)\,dx_t\), a little less noisy than \(p_t\) and not Gaussian at all (right). Step after step, the round noise becomes the roll.

The sampler then chains these steps, \(x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t,t) + \sigma_t z\) with fresh noise \(z\): one Gaussian per step, and the chain lands on a cat or a dog. So diffusion never asks a network for many answers at once. (The same averages also drive a deterministic sampler, one map from noise to data; that is Part 5.)

Figure 3. One step back, the noise decides. A 1-D toy with two kinds of data, cats and dogs, and one noisy \(x_t\). Where was \(x_t\) before? By Bayes rule the answer (red fill) is the noise window, where the noise lets it have been (gray), times where data is common at that noise level (red line), each scaled to height 1. (1) All the way back, 32 steps of noise make a wide window and both hills show through: two answers, whose average (gray ring) is no data. (2) One step back, the window is narrow and the data under it is nearly flat, so the answer is the window’s own shape, one Gaussian (off by 0.1% of the mass), and its average (red dot) is the answer.
Figure 4. One Gaussian per point. The swiss roll of the 2015 paper. (1) One step, at the moment of the third panel on the right: each point \(x_t\) (black dot) lands in its own small round Gaussian (red, to scale, \(\beta = 0.02\)), centered a little closer to the roll; the network predicts only that center. (2) All steps: the distribution of \(x_t\) from pure noise \(x_T\) (gray) to the roll \(x_0\) (red), along the inference axis. Between neighbouring steps, each distribution is the sum of the small Gaussians of all the points of the one before (bottom formula).

Problem. From samples \(x_1,\dots,x_N \sim p_{\text{data}}\), build a \(p_\theta\) we can (1) sample, (2) evaluate, and (3) fit from the samples alone, flexible enough for data like images.

1. What is a generative model actually trying to do?

It is trying to build a probability model: to describe the distribution the data came from, with a distribution we can compute with.

We never see \(p_{\text{data}}\). We see samples. A generative model is a parametric distribution \(p_\theta\) that we try to make behave like \(p_{\text{data}}\), and “behave like” hides three requirements that drive everything else:

  1. Sampling \(x\sim p_\theta\): produce new data. This is the reason the model exists.
  2. Evaluation \(p_\theta(x)\): say how plausible a given \(x\) is. Needed for anomaly detection, compression, model comparison, and for computing posteriors.
  3. Training \(\max_\theta\ \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x)]\): fit \(\theta\) from samples alone. This is the same as minimizing \(\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_\theta)\).
Figure 5. Tractable and flexible. (A) We only have the blue samples; the gray dashed density is unknown; the orange curve is the model we fit. (B) One Gaussian is closed-form everywhere but puts its mode where there is no data; any \(\phi(x)\) fits the shape but its normalizer \(Z\) cannot be computed. (C) Diffusion stacks many small Gaussian steps from data to noise and learns the reverse. One step is simple; the chain is flexible.

Figure 5 is the map for this post: A is what we want, B is why it is hard, C is how diffusion answers. The next sections zoom into each panel.

2. Why can’t we just use a flexible neural network as the density?

Because a flexible function is not a distribution until you divide by its integral over the whole space, and that integral, \(Z\), is exactly what you cannot compute.

Deep learning has one tool, function approximation: a network \(f_\theta\) can be bent toward almost any function, as long as we can write a loss and follow its gradient. But a distribution is not a function with labeled targets; no image comes stamped with its probability. So we first have to decide which function the network stands in for: the density \(p_\theta(x)\) itself, an unnormalized energy, a map from noise to data, or the conditional of each pixel given the ones before it. That decision is the whole of probabilistic modeling, and this section takes the most direct option, the density itself.

Take any non-negative \(\phi(x)\), a neural network if you like, and set \(p(x)=\phi(x)/Z\) with

\[Z=\int_{\mathbb{R}^D}\phi(x)\,dx .\]

For an image, \(D=3\times256\times256\). There is no closed form, and numerical integration dies of the curse of dimensionality. Without \(Z\), none of the three requirements of Section 1 can be met directly: sampling, evaluation and training all fall back on MCMC. That is the energy-based model’s thirty-year problem, and it is the right-hand side of Figure 5B.

The left-hand side is the opposite extreme. A single Gaussian has \(Z=(2\pi)^{D/2}|\Sigma|^{1/2}\) and samples in one line. It also ignores whether the data is a ring, two blobs, or anything else, and puts its mode in the empty middle.

The axis under Figure 5B, from tractable to flexible, is the trade the first diffusion paper names in its opening sentence, Sohl-Dickstein et al. (ICML 2015): “Historically, probabilistic models suffer from a tradeoff between two conflicting objectives: tractability and flexibility.” Every model family before diffusion picked a point on it.

3. How did other families get around \(Z\), and what did each give up?

Each found a different way to avoid computing \(Z\), and each paid with one of the things we wanted: exact likelihood, diversity, speed, or architectural freedom.

Goodfellow’s NIPS 2016 tutorial sorts generative models by whether they write the density down (explicit vs. implicit) and, if so, whether they compute it exactly or approximately. Re-reading that taxonomy with \(Z\) as the axis gives the table below, and Figure 0 is the same table as a picture.

4. Where do prior and posterior enter, and why is the posterior the hard part?

In any latent-variable model the prior is easy and the posterior is the obstacle. VAEs approximate it; diffusion designs the problem so the posterior is a Gaussian you can write down.

A latent-variable model samples \(z\) from a simple prior \(p(z)\) and decodes \(x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)\). To train it you need the reverse direction, the posterior \(p(z\mid x)\), and Bayes’ rule puts \(p(x)=\int p(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz\) in the denominator. That is the same kind of integral as \(Z\) in Section 2, so the posterior is unknown too.

Figure 8. Posterior by design. (a) Prior times likelihood gives the shape of the posterior; its height needs the integral \(p(x)\). (b) With a nonlinear decoder the true posterior bends like a banana; a VAE covers it with a Gaussian \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) and the gap is \(\log p(x)-\mathrm{ELBO}\). (c) Diffusion fixes the forward process to parameter-free Gaussian noising, so the posterior \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t,x_0)\) comes out as a closed-form Gaussian. The network only has to match it.

Figure 8(c) is the most important picture in this post. Diffusion’s latent variables are the noisy copies \(x_1,\dots,x_T\), its prior is \(x_T\approx\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), and its forward process has no parameters. Because of that, the posterior is computable and training turns from “approximate an unknown posterior” into “regress onto a known Gaussian”. The 2015 paper says it in one line:

“Estimating small perturbations is more tractable than explicitly describing the full distribution with a single, non-analytically-normalizable, potential function.”

5. So what exactly does diffusion do differently?

One step is a Gaussian, so it is tractable; a thousand steps composed are flexible. Instead of picking a point on the tractable-to-flexible axis, diffusion takes both ends.

Look at Figure 5C again. Adding a little Gaussian noise, \(q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})\), is a Gaussian. The key fact (Feller, 1949; Figure 3) is that when the noise is small enough, the reverse \(q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) is also a Gaussian, so we may model it as \(p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)\) and only learn its mean and variance.

  • One step: closed-form probability and sampling, so tractable.
  • \(T\) steps composed: reaches any smooth distribution, so flexible.
  • Training: a lower bound (ELBO) that is a sum, over steps, of KL divergences between the known posterior and the model’s Gaussian. This is the same objective DDPM uses in 2020.

The 2015 paper lists four things it wants at once: “extreme flexibility in model structure, exact sampling, easy multiplication with other distributions, cheap evaluation of log likelihood.” That is Section 1’s three requirements plus conditioning, which is the last row of Figure 0.
